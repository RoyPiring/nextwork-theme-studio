The NextWork catalogue
Project GeneratorExpansion

Your own island across the water: projects you generated yourself, in series of your own. The shared world is the ninety everyone can do, so every learner's town means the same thing; the island is yours alone.

Every series is one building on your land. It goes up when you finish the first part and grows with each one after — half the parts is tier two, all of them is tier three and a gem. XP is by rarity: the fewer people who have done a project, the more it is worth.

The loop

Tick a step on a project → XP and coins → a piece of the component you are building. Finish the project → a gem, and the component goes onto your land, on a plot you choose. Nothing else is ever asked for; the site already knows all of it.

Growth is cumulative, not calendar

The thresholds are set so that finishing the beginner path is a homestead, one full series is a village, and the whole catalogue is a metropolis.

A learner who does two projects has a homestead. A learner who does twenty has a town. Nobody waits a year for anything, and nothing is lost by walking away for one.

Builders

You start with one. A second arrives at 700 XP and a third at 1,500. On a build, each builder lays a piece per step — so with three builders a seven-piece component is up in three steps rather than seven. Builders live in the hut on your land; it is the one building nobody has to earn.

Two views, like Clash of Clans

  • My World is your base, close up. Every project you have finished is a building at its own level, grouped by series into districts, inside a wall that grows with your tier. The Town Hall in the middle is the tier. This is the view you look at.
  • World is the map: HQ, the roads, other learners, your land as one place among many, with one building per series so it reads at a distance. This is the view you look around.

A building's level is the project's rarity. The fewer people who have finished it, the higher the level it stands at — so a base full of level-five buildings says something a base full of level-ones does not.

The open world

  • Your land is a region of the map that is yours. It starts barren. Every building you place greens the ground around it, so a well-built plot is visibly alive and an empty one is visibly waiting.
  • NextWork HQ is the hub town up the road: the headquarters building, a plaza with a fountain, and the notice board. Press Visit HQ to go and look. One day it is where you see events, leaderboards, and who is online.
  • Other learners build next door. You can see their land and what they have made, with a nameplate. Nothing of theirs can be touched from yours.

Building the component

The Build tab is where the thing gets made, one piece per step, then placed. Every project declares what it builds — a house, a car, a data centre — and how:

  • Waterfall: foundation, frame, walls, roof, windows, door, garden. Nothing is usable until the end.
  • Agile: shed, cabin, cottage, house, +garden, +garage, +second storey. Something whole at every step.

Same XP, same finished thing. A different feeling in the middle — which is what the two methods feel like on a real team, and a thing worth teaching.

The expansion

The Project Generator is a second island across the water with its own bridge. Your own generated projects live there - 112 of them at last count, in 48 series - and they build the island, not the town. The main world stays the shared ninety, so that every learner's town means the same thing when two of them stand side by side. Whether the bridge opens, and what unlocks it, is NextWork's call.

What the extension can actually read

Everything here runs inside the existing extension's rules: no network, no account, nothing leaves the machine. The tag and title come from the page heading, ticked steps from the page's own checkboxes, and time on the page from the focus timer that already exists. State lives where the themes live.

The one thing this demo shows that the extension cannot do alone is other learners and the hub. Those need NextWork to hold the world. That is the conversation to have with them.

Art

Isometric, one light from the top-left, shadows to the front. Every building is drawn in code — brick and siding on the walls, shingle rows on the roofs, framed windows with sills and lit glass, doors with a step, chimneys, gutters, fences, lamp posts, rooftop plant. It is a long way past a box, and still a long way short of an artist's tileset. The geometry is what it would be; the tileset is a designer's month.
